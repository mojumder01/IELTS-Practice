Green Building Trends

How architects are rethinking the way buildings use energy and materials

A

Green building refers to the practice of designing, constructing and operating structures in ways that reduce their impact on the environment across their entire life cycle. While early efforts in the 1970s focused narrowly on cutting energy bills after the oil crisis1, the movement has since broadened to include water use, material sourcing and the health of the people inside.

B

Energy efficiency nevertheless remains the primary objective for most architects.2 Heating and cooling account for the largest share of a typical building’s energy demand, so designers concentrate first on the building envelope6: the walls, roof and windows that separate inside from outside. High-performance insulation and triple glazing can sharply reduce heating requirements in cold climates.

C

Orientation is a cheaper but often overlooked tool. A building whose longest wall faces the sun can capture winter warmth7 through its windows, while overhanging roofs block the higher summer sun. Such passive design costs little to implement at the planning stage but is difficult and expensive to add later4.

D

Materials have attracted growing attention. Concrete and steel carry a heavy embodied carbon cost8, released during their manufacture long before a building opens. Some architects are turning to engineered timber, which stores carbon rather than emitting it, although critics question whether forests could supply enough wood if the practice became widespread.

E

Certification schemes have helped turn these ideas into market value. Buildings that meet recognised green standards often command higher rents, and in several cities public projects must now achieve a minimum rating. Yet some researchers warn that a certificate measures design intentions9, not actual performance once the building is occupied.
